Unit 1

Organization of Life

NGSS Standards: LS1-1, LS1-2 · Cells, Structure & Function, Levels of Organization

1.1 Characteristics of Life

All living organisms share a set of fundamental characteristics that distinguish them from non-living matter. Understanding these characteristics is the foundation of all biological study.

Organization
Living things are made of one or more cells, arranged in a highly ordered structure from atoms → molecules → organelles → cells → tissues → organs → organ systems → organisms.
Metabolism
The sum of all chemical reactions in an organism, including both building up (anabolism) and breaking down (catabolism) of substances.
Homeostasis
The ability to maintain a stable internal environment despite changes in the external environment (e.g., regulating body temperature at 37°C).
Growth & Development
Living things increase in size and complexity. Growth is directed by genetic information (DNA) and follows predictable patterns.
Reproduction
Living things produce offspring. Reproduction can be sexual (two parents, genetic variation) or asexual (one parent, genetically identical offspring).
Response to Stimuli
Organisms detect and respond to changes in their environment. Examples: plants growing toward light (phototropism), pupils dilating in the dark.
Evolutionary Adaptation
Populations of organisms change over time through natural selection, becoming better suited to their environment.
Energy Processing
All organisms require and use energy. Autotrophs make their own food; heterotrophs consume other organisms for energy.
Memory Trick — MRS.GREN
Movement · Respiration · Sensitivity · Growth · Reproduction · Excretion · Nutrition — the 7 life processes shared by all living things.

1.2 Levels of Biological Organization

Life is organized in a hierarchical manner, with each level building on the one below it.

Detailed Breakdown of Each Level

  • Atom: The basic unit of matter (e.g., carbon, hydrogen, oxygen, nitrogen). Biology is built on four key elements: C, H, O, N (plus P, S).
  • Molecule: Two or more atoms bonded together. Key biological molecules include water (H₂O), glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆), DNA, and proteins.
  • Organelle: Specialized structures within cells that perform specific functions (e.g., mitochondria produce energy; ribosomes make proteins).
  • Cell: The fundamental unit of life. The smallest structure capable of performing all life functions.
  • Tissue: A group of similar cells working together (e.g., muscle tissue, nerve tissue, epithelial tissue).
  • Organ: A structure composed of two or more tissue types working together (e.g., the heart contains muscle, nerve, and connective tissue).
  • Organ System: A group of organs that cooperate to perform major body functions (e.g., the cardiovascular system = heart + blood vessels + blood).
  • Organism: An individual living thing (e.g., a human, a dog, a tree, a bacterium).
  • Population: All individuals of the same species living in the same area at the same time.
  • Community: All populations of different species living in the same area.
  • Ecosystem: A community plus its abiotic (non-living) environment (soil, water, sunlight, temperature).
  • Biosphere: All ecosystems on Earth combined — the zone of life on our planet.

1.3 Cell Theory

Cell theory is one of the most fundamental principles of biology, developed through centuries of scientific observation.

The Three Principles of Cell Theory
  1. All living things are made of one or more cells.
  2. The cell is the basic unit of structure and function in all living organisms.
  3. All cells come from pre-existing cells (omnis cellula e cellula — Virchow, 1855).

Unit 1 Key Takeaways

  • Life is organized hierarchically: atom → molecule → organelle → cell → tissue → organ → system → organism → population → community → ecosystem → biosphere
  • Cell theory: all life is made of cells; cells are the basic unit; cells come from cells
  • Prokaryotes (bacteria) lack a membrane-bound nucleus; eukaryotes have a true nucleus

C — Sort, modify, and package proteins for transport. Think of the Golgi apparatus as the cell's "post office." B — Maintain a stable internal environment. Multiple Choice

  • Only animal cells are considered true cells
  • All cells come from pre-existing cells

C — All cells come from pre-existing cells. This is the third tenet of cell theory, established by Virchow in 1855. Multiple Choice

10. A lysosome contains digestive enzymes that break down cellular waste. If a lysosome's membrane ruptured, what would most likely happen?

  • Bacteria only

Multiple Choice

18. A student observes a cell that has a large central vacuole, chloroplasts, and a cell wall. This cell is MOST LIKELY from a:

  • Bacterial cell
  • Animal cell
  • Plant cell
  • Fungal cell
Answer & Explanation
C — Plant cell. Multiple Choice

19. Which statement BEST explains why cells are small?

  • Cells need to fit inside organs
  • Smaller cells have a higher surface area-to-volume ratio, allowing efficient exchange of materials
  • Large cells require too much DNA
  • Cells divide before they get too old
Answer & Explanation
B — Smaller cells have a higher surface area-to-volume ratio, allowing efficient exchange of materials. As a cell grows larger, its volume increases faster than its surface area (SA:V ratio decreases). If cells became too large, the membrane surface area would be insufficient to move enough nutrients in and waste out to supply the growing volume. Staying small maintains efficient transport.
Multiple Choice

20. Which pair of organelles are BOTH involved in protein production and processing?

  • Mitochondria and chloroplast
  • Ribosome and rough endoplasmic reticulum
  • Lysosome and vacuole
  • Centriole and cytoskeleton
Answer & Explanation
B — Ribosome and rough endoplasmic reticulum. Ribosomes synthesize proteins by translating mRNA. Ribosomes attached to the rough ER insert newly made proteins into the ER lumen, where they are folded and begin initial processing before being sent to the Golgi. These two organelles work together as the first step in the secretory pathway.

Section B — True or False (5 Questions)

True / False

21. All prokaryotic cells have a cell wall.

Answer
FALSE. While most prokaryotes (bacteria) have cell walls made of peptidoglycan, some bacteria (like Mycoplasma) lack cell walls entirely, which is why they have unusual shapes and are resistant to antibiotics that target cell wall synthesis (like penicillin).
True / False

22. The biosphere is the most inclusive level of biological organization.

Answer
TRUE. The biosphere encompasses all living organisms and their environments on Earth — it includes all ecosystems on land, in water, and in the atmosphere. It is the highest and most comprehensive level in the hierarchy of biological organization.
True / False

23. Animal cells have a large central vacuole for water storage.

Answer
FALSE. Large central vacuoles are a feature of plant cells, where they store water, nutrients, and waste, and help maintain cell rigidity (turgor pressure). Animal cells do have small vacuoles, but they are not large or central, and they serve different functions.
True / False

24. Viruses are considered living organisms because they can reproduce.

Answer
FALSE. Viruses are generally NOT considered living organisms. Although they can replicate, they cannot do so independently — they require a host cell's machinery. Viruses also lack cells (violating cell theory), cannot carry out metabolism on their own, and do not maintain homeostasis.
True / False

25. The cytoskeleton provides structural support and helps with cell movement.

Answer
TRUE. The cytoskeleton is a network of protein fibers (microfilaments of actin, intermediate filaments, and microtubules) that gives the cell its shape, anchors organelles, facilitates intracellular transport, and is essential for cell movement and division.

Section C — Short Answer (7 Questions)

Short Answer

26. Explain the concept "form follows function" using the mitochondrion as an example.

Model Answer
The mitochondrion's structure is perfectly adapted to its function of producing ATP. It has a double membrane: the smooth outer membrane separates it from the cytoplasm, while the inner membrane is extensively folded into structures called cristae. These folds dramatically increase the surface area available for the electron transport chain proteins, which pump protons to drive ATP synthesis. A larger surface area means more ATP can be produced per unit time. Additionally, the space between the two membranes (intermembrane space) accumulates protons, creating the electrochemical gradient needed to power ATP synthase. The enclosed matrix contains the enzymes for the Krebs cycle and the mitochondrion's own DNA — all contained within this organelle.
Short Answer

27. A scientist discovers a new single-celled organism. It has a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles but no cell wall. What domain does it most likely belong to, and what type of organism might it be?

Model Answer
This organism most likely belongs to Domain Eukarya because it has a membrane-bound nucleus and membrane-bound organelles (features of eukaryotes). The absence of a cell wall rules out plants (cellulose wall), fungi (chitin wall), and bacteria (peptidoglycan wall). The organism is most likely a protist (kingdom Protista) — a diverse group of single-celled eukaryotes that lack cell walls and can be heterotrophic (like amoeba) or autotrophic (like some algae). It could also theoretically be an animal cell, but animals are multicellular, so a single-celled non-walled eukaryote fits the protist classification best.
Short Answer

28. Describe the pathway a secretory protein takes from synthesis to secretion outside the cell.

Model Answer
This pathway is called the secretory pathway:
1. Ribosome (on rough ER) — protein is synthesized from mRNA
2. Rough ER — protein enters the ER lumen; begins folding; receives initial modifications (e.g., addition of carbohydrate groups)
3. Transport vesicle — protein is packaged into a vesicle that buds off the ER
4. Golgi apparatus — vesicle fuses with Golgi (cis face); protein moves through Golgi stacks; further modification occurs (glycosylation, proteolytic cleavage); protein is sorted and tagged
5. Secretory vesicle — protein exits Golgi (trans face) in a vesicle
6. Plasma membrane — vesicle fuses with cell membrane (exocytosis); protein is released outside the cell
Short Answer

29. What is homeostasis? Give two specific examples of how the human body maintains homeostasis.

Model Answer
Homeostasis is the maintenance of a stable internal environment within narrow ranges despite changes in external conditions. It relies on feedback mechanisms (usually negative feedback).

Example 1 — Temperature regulation: Normal body temperature is ~37°C. When temperature rises, the hypothalamus triggers sweating (evaporative cooling) and vasodilation (blood vessels near skin widen to release heat). When temperature drops, shivering generates heat and vasoconstriction (blood vessels narrow) conserves it.

Example 2 — Blood glucose: After a meal, blood glucose rises. The pancreas releases insulin, signaling cells to take up glucose and the liver to convert it to glycogen (storage). When blood glucose falls too low, the pancreas releases glucagon, signaling the liver to break down glycogen and release glucose back into the blood.
Short Answer

30. Why is the discovery that "all cells come from pre-existing cells" (Virchow) considered so important in biology?

Model Answer
Virchow's principle was revolutionary because it disproved spontaneous generation — the ancient idea that living organisms could arise from non-living matter (e.g., that maggots spontaneously appeared in meat). By establishing that every cell must come from a pre-existing cell, it: (1) provided a mechanistic understanding of how life perpetuates itself; (2) laid the groundwork for germ theory (diseases are caused by pre-existing microorganisms, not spontaneous appearance); (3) established reproduction as a fundamental property of cells; and (4) unified all of biology under a single framework — every organism alive today is the product of an unbroken chain of cell divisions stretching back to the origin of life on Earth.
Short Answer

31. Compare and contrast the function of the large central vacuole in a plant cell versus a lysosome in an animal cell.

Model Answer
Similarities: Both are membrane-bound compartments that help manage cellular contents.

Large central vacuole (plant): Can occupy up to 90% of cell volume. Its primary functions are: (1) water storage and turgor pressure maintenance — keeps plant cells firm/rigid; (2) storage of nutrients, pigments (anthocyanins), and metabolic waste; (3) defense — can contain toxic compounds that deter herbivores. It is slightly acidic but not as acidic as lysosomes.

Lysosome (animal): Much smaller. Contains powerful hydrolytic (digestive) enzymes at very low pH (~4.5). Functions: (1) digestion of cellular waste and damaged organelles (autophagy); (2) digestion of food particles engulfed by the cell; (3) breakdown of foreign invaders (bacteria). Lysosomes are the cell's "recycling center."
Short Answer

32. List and briefly explain all eight characteristics of life.

Model Answer
1. Organization: Living things have highly ordered structures from atoms to organisms.
2. Metabolism: Chemical reactions in the body (both building up and breaking down molecules).
3. Homeostasis: Maintaining a stable internal environment despite external changes.
4. Growth & Development: Organisms increase in size/complexity following a genetically programmed pattern.
5. Reproduction: Organisms produce offspring (sexual or asexual).
6. Response to stimuli: Detecting and reacting to environmental changes.
7. Energy use: All organisms require energy; autotrophs make it via photosynthesis, heterotrophs consume other organisms.
8. Evolutionary adaptation: Populations change over generations through natural selection, becoming better suited to their environment.

Section D — Essay Questions (3 Questions)

Essay

33. You are designing a science exhibit for middle school students about cell organelles. Choose FIVE organelles and explain, for each one: (a) what it looks like, (b) what its job is, and (c) what would happen to the cell if this organelle stopped working.

Model Answer (Guide)
1. Nucleus — (a) Spherical, surrounded by a double membrane with pores; contains chromatin. (b) Stores DNA and directs all cellular activities; the "brain" of the cell. (c) Without a nucleus, the cell cannot replicate its DNA, transcribe genes, or repair itself — it would eventually die.

2. Mitochondria — (a) Oval-shaped with a smooth outer and folded inner membrane (cristae). (b) Produces ATP via cellular respiration; the "powerhouse." (c) Without mitochondria, cells would lack sufficient ATP for active processes — they could only do anaerobic glycolysis; muscle cells would fail, neurons would die.

3. Ribosome — (a) Tiny two-subunit structures made of protein and RNA; found free or attached to rough ER. (b) Synthesizes proteins by translating mRNA. (c) Without ribosomes, no proteins could be made — enzymes, structural proteins, and signaling molecules would all cease to be produced, causing total cellular failure.

4. Golgi Apparatus — (a) Stack of flat membrane sacs, like a stack of pancakes. (b) Receives, sorts, modifies, and packages proteins for delivery. (c) Without Golgi, proteins would be made but not properly processed or sorted — secreted proteins would accumulate in the ER; the cell's protein delivery system would break down.

5. Chloroplast (plant cells) — (a) Oval, double-membraned organelle with internal thylakoid membranes stacked into grana. (b) Converts light energy to chemical energy (glucose) via photosynthesis. (c) Without chloroplasts, the plant could not make its own food and would have to rely entirely on cellular respiration of stored reserves — it would eventually starve unless supplied with sugar externally.
Essay

34. Compare and contrast prokaryotic and eukaryotic cells. In your answer, address: structure, size, reproduction, examples, and evolutionary relationship.

Model Answer (Guide)
Prokaryotic cells are the oldest and simplest form of life (evolved ~3.5 billion years ago). They are small (1–10 µm), lack a membrane-bound nucleus (their DNA is in a nucleoid region), and lack membrane-bound organelles. They reproduce asexually by binary fission, which is very rapid. Examples include bacteria and archaea.

Eukaryotic cells are larger (10–100 µm) and more complex, with a true nucleus enclosed in a double membrane, and a variety of specialized organelles (mitochondria, ER, Golgi, etc.). They reproduce via mitosis (asexual) or meiosis + fertilization (sexual). Examples include all plants, animals, fungi, and protists.

Evolutionary relationship: Eukaryotes are believed to have evolved from prokaryotes via endosymbiosis (Margulis hypothesis). According to this theory, mitochondria were once free-living aerobic bacteria engulfed by a host prokaryote — evidence includes the fact that mitochondria and chloroplasts have their own circular DNA, their own ribosomes (70S, like bacteria), reproduce independently by binary fission, and are surrounded by a double membrane.

Key similarities: Both have DNA as genetic material, ribosomes for protein synthesis, cell membranes, and carry out the same basic metabolic reactions.
Essay

35. Explain the levels of biological organization from atom to biosphere. Why is this hierarchical organization important for understanding biology?

Model Answer (Guide)
Biology is organized hierarchically because complex systems are built from simpler components. Starting at the smallest scale: atoms (C, H, O, N) bond to form molecules (water, glucose, DNA, proteins). Molecules are organized within cells into organelles, and organelles collectively comprise a cell — the basic unit of life. In multicellular organisms, similar cells group into tissues (muscle, nerve), tissues form organs (heart, lung), and organs cooperate as organ systems (cardiovascular, respiratory). All systems together constitute an organism.

At larger scales: a group of the same species in one place forms a population; multiple populations of different species form a community; a community with its abiotic environment forms an ecosystem; and all ecosystems together constitute the biosphere.

Why it matters: This hierarchical view allows biologists to study life at different levels of complexity with appropriate tools and frameworks. A molecular biologist studies enzymes and DNA; a cell biologist studies organelles; an ecologist studies populations and ecosystems. Understanding how lower-level processes (enzyme activity) produce higher-level emergent properties (organism behavior or ecosystem stability) is fundamental to all of biology. It also helps explain how a change at one level (e.g., a DNA mutation) can cascade to affect all higher levels (altered protein → disease → changed organism → impact on population).

2.1 The Cell Membrane — Fluid Mosaic Model

The plasma (cell) membrane is one of the most important structures in biology. It defines the boundary of the cell and regulates everything that enters or exits. The currently accepted model is the Fluid Mosaic Model (Singer & Nicolson, 1972).

Why "Fluid Mosaic"?
"Fluid" — the phospholipids and proteins can move laterally within the bilayer (like icebergs floating in oil). This fluidity is essential for membrane function.
"Mosaic" — the membrane is a patchwork of many different types of proteins embedded in the lipid bilayer, giving it a mosaic-like appearance under an electron microscope.

Selective Permeability

The plasma membrane is selectively permeable — it allows some substances to pass freely while restricting or blocking others:

  • Freely pass (no energy needed): Small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂, N₂); small polar molecules (water — H₂O, somewhat)
  • Restricted or require transport proteins: Ions (Na⁺, K⁺, Cl⁻); large polar molecules (glucose, amino acids)
  • Cannot pass without endocytosis/exocytosis: Large macromolecules (proteins, polysaccharides)

2.2 Passive Transport — Movement Without Energy

Passive transport moves substances across the membrane along their concentration gradient (from high → low concentration). It does NOT require ATP.

Diffusion

Diffusion is the net movement of particles from an area of high concentration to an area of low concentration (down the concentration gradient) until equilibrium is reached. Driven by the random motion of particles.

Examples: O₂ diffusing from blood into tissues; CO₂ diffusing from tissues into blood; perfume spreading through a room.

Osmosis

Osmosis is the diffusion of water across a selectively permeable membrane from a region of low solute concentration (high water concentration) to a region of high solute concentration (low water concentration).

Key Osmosis Terms

  • Hypotonic solution: Lower solute concentration than inside the cell → water moves IN → cell swells → may lyse (burst) in animal cells; becomes turgid in plant cells (beneficial)
  • Hypertonic solution: Higher solute concentration than inside the cell → water moves OUT → cell shrinks/crenates (animal) or plasmolysis (plant)
  • Isotonic solution: Same solute concentration as inside the cell → no net water movement → cell maintains normal size
  • Turgor pressure: Pressure exerted by water against the cell wall in plant cells; keeps plants firm/upright
  • Plasmolysis: When a plant cell loses so much water that the plasma membrane pulls away from the cell wall; plant wilts

Facilitated Diffusion

Facilitated diffusion moves substances down their concentration gradient (high → low) using transport proteins. No ATP required, but proteins provide a pathway for substances that cannot diffuse directly through the lipid bilayer.

  • Channel proteins: Form water-filled pores; allow specific ions or small molecules to pass (e.g., aquaporins for water, ion channels for Na⁺/K⁺)
  • Carrier proteins: Bind to a specific molecule, change shape, and release the molecule on the other side (e.g., GLUT transporters for glucose)

2.3 Active Transport — Movement Against the Gradient

Active transport moves substances from low to high concentration (against the gradient) — the opposite of diffusion. This requires ATP energy and specific transport proteins (pumps).

Primary Active Transport

Uses ATP directly. The most important example is the Sodium-Potassium Pump (Na⁺/K⁺ ATPase):

  • Pumps 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per cycle, using 1 ATP
  • Creates electrochemical gradients essential for nerve impulses and muscle contractions
  • Maintains normal cell volume (if Na⁺ accumulated, water would follow by osmosis, swelling the cell)

Secondary Active Transport

Uses the energy stored in an ion gradient (created by primary active transport) to move another substance. Example: glucose-sodium symport in intestinal cells uses the Na⁺ gradient to pull glucose into cells against glucose's concentration gradient.

2.4 The Cell Cycle & Mitosis

Cells reproduce by dividing. The cell cycle is the ordered sequence of events from cell formation to cell division. It ensures that DNA is accurately copied and distributed to daughter cells.

Phases of the Cell Cycle

Interphase (G1 + S + G2) — the cell prepares for division:

  • G1 (Gap 1/Growth 1): Cell grows in size; synthesizes proteins and organelles; normal cell functions; the cell "decides" whether to divide (checkpoint)
  • S (Synthesis): DNA replication — each chromosome is duplicated to form two sister chromatids joined at the centromere; centrosomes are duplicated
  • G2 (Gap 2/Growth 2): Cell continues to grow; duplicates organelles; repairs DNA errors; prepares mitotic spindle proteins (G2 checkpoint)

Cancer — When the Cell Cycle Goes Wrong

The cell cycle is tightly regulated by checkpoints (G1, G2, and spindle assembly checkpoint). These are controlled by proteins called cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs).

  • Proto-oncogenes: Normal genes that promote cell growth. When mutated → oncogenes that cause uncontrolled division.
  • Tumor suppressor genes: Normally slow or stop cell division (e.g., p53, Rb). Mutations inactivate them, allowing uncontrolled growth.
  • Cancer = uncontrolled cell division caused by accumulated mutations in cell cycle control genes. Tumor cells can metastasize (spread) to other tissues.

Unit 2 Key Takeaways

  • The cell membrane is a fluid mosaic of phospholipids, proteins, cholesterol, and glycoproteins — selectively permeable
  • Passive transport (diffusion, osmosis, facilitated diffusion) requires NO energy — moves down concentration gradient
  • Active transport requires ATP — moves substances AGAINST the gradient; includes pumps and bulk transport
  • Osmosis: water moves from low solute → high solute concentration; hypotonic swells cells, hypertonic shrinks them
  • The cell cycle: G1 → S (DNA replication) → G2 → Mitosis (PMAT) → Cytokinesis → 2 identical daughter cells
  • Checkpoints regulated by cyclins and CDKs; mutations in these cause cancer

Section B — Short Answer

Short Answer

11. Explain why the sodium-potassium pump is critical for nerve cell function.

Model Answer
The Na⁺/K⁺ pump maintains the resting membrane potential of neurons by constantly pumping 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in (using 1 ATP per cycle), keeping the inside of the cell negatively charged relative to the outside. This electrochemical gradient is the source of potential energy for nerve impulse transmission. When a neuron is stimulated, Na⁺ channels open and Na⁺ rushes in (depolarization) — this is the action potential. After the impulse, K⁺ rushes out and the pump restores the resting state. Without the pump, the gradient would collapse and neurons could not fire.
Short Answer

12. What is a checkpoint in the cell cycle, and why are checkpoints important for preventing cancer?

Model Answer
Checkpoints are regulatory control points in the cell cycle that assess whether conditions are suitable for the cell to proceed to the next phase. The three major checkpoints are: (1) G1 checkpoint (Is the cell large enough? Is DNA intact? Are growth signals present?); (2) G2 checkpoint (Was DNA replicated completely and correctly? Is the cell large enough?); and (3) Spindle assembly checkpoint (Are all chromosomes attached to spindle fibers?). These are controlled by cyclins and CDKs. If a checkpoint detects a problem, it halts the cycle for repair, or triggers apoptosis (programmed cell death). Mutations in checkpoint genes (like p53 — the "guardian of the genome") allow cells with damaged DNA to continue dividing, accumulating more mutations and potentially developing into cancer.
Short Answer

13. Describe what happens to an animal cell and a plant cell placed in pure water (a hypotonic solution). Why are the results different?

Model Answer
Animal cell in pure water: Water rushes INTO the cell by osmosis (pure water = 0 solute outside; cytoplasm has high solute). The cell swells significantly and may lyse (burst), because the plasma membrane has no rigid support to resist expansion.

Plant cell in pure water: Water also rushes in, and the cell swells and becomes turgid. However, the rigid cell wall pushes back against the expanding membrane, creating turgor pressure. The cell does NOT burst — it becomes firm. This turgor pressure is actually desirable for plants; it keeps them upright (wilting = loss of turgor).

Why different? The cell wall in plant cells provides structural resistance to over-expansion. Animal cells lack a cell wall, so there is nothing to oppose the osmotic pressure when water enters, causing them to lyse.
Short Answer

14. Compare simple diffusion with facilitated diffusion. When would a cell use each type?

Model Answer
Simple diffusion: Movement of small, nonpolar (or very small polar) molecules directly through the phospholipid bilayer, down the concentration gradient. No proteins needed. Examples: O₂, CO₂, N₂, ethanol, steroids. Used when molecules are small enough and nonpolar enough to pass through the hydrophobic interior of the membrane.

Facilitated diffusion: Movement of larger or polar/charged molecules down their concentration gradient, but using specific transport proteins (channels or carriers). Still requires NO ATP. Examples: glucose (via GLUT carriers), Na⁺/K⁺/Cl⁻ ions (via ion channels), water (via aquaporins). Used when molecules cannot cross the hydrophobic lipid bilayer on their own but must still move with the gradient.
Essay

15. You are given four cells in different stages of mitosis. Describe each stage (Prophase, Metaphase, Anaphase, Telophase) in detail: what you would see under a microscope, and what molecular events are occurring. Then explain why mitosis must produce genetically identical cells.

Model Answer
Prophase: Under the microscope, you would see the chromatin coiling and condensing into distinct, visible chromosomes. Each chromosome consists of two sister chromatids joined at the centromere. The nucleolus disappears. The nuclear envelope begins to break down. Centrioles (in animal cells) move to opposite poles, and spindle fibers (made of microtubules) begin to extend from them toward the chromosomes. This is the longest phase of mitosis.

Metaphase: Chromosomes are maximally condensed (easiest to count!). Spindle fibers attach to the kinetochores (protein structures at centromeres) and align chromosomes precisely along the cell's equatorial plane — the metaphase plate. This alignment ensures each daughter cell will receive one copy of each chromosome.

Anaphase: The centromeres split simultaneously. Sister chromatids are pulled to opposite poles by the shortening of kinetochore spindle fibers. The cell elongates as polar spindle fibers push poles apart. Each pole now has a complete, identical set of chromosomes.

Telophase: Chromosomes arrive at the poles and begin to decondense back into chromatin. New nuclear envelopes form around each set. The spindle breaks down. The nucleolus reappears in each nucleus. Cytokinesis overlaps with telophase: in animals, a cleavage furrow (actin ring) pinches the cell in two; in plants, a cell plate of vesicles forms down the middle and expands into a new cell wall.

Why genetically identical? Mitosis must produce identical cells because it is used for growth (adding identical body cells), tissue repair (replacing damaged cells with identical copies), and asexual reproduction (producing offspring with the same genetic information). DNA replication during S phase creates exact copies of every chromosome. The precise alignment and separation mechanisms ensure each daughter cell receives exactly one complete set. If errors occurred, daughter cells might have too many or too few chromosomes (aneuploidy), which can cause cell death or cancer.

3.1 ATP — The Cell's Energy Currency

All living organisms require energy to carry out life processes. The universal energy currency of cells is ATP (Adenosine Triphosphate).

Structure of ATP

  • Adenosine: Adenine (nitrogenous base) + ribose (5-carbon sugar)
  • Three phosphate groups (α, β, γ): Connected in a chain by high-energy phosphate bonds
  • When the terminal phosphate bond is hydrolyzed: ATP → ADP + Pᵢ + Energy
  • Energy released is used to power cellular work (active transport, muscle contraction, biosynthesis)
  • ADP is recycled back to ATP by adding phosphate (using energy from cellular respiration or photosynthesis)
ATP Facts
A human at rest uses ~40 kg of ATP per day — but the body contains only ~250g of ATP at any moment. This means each ATP molecule is recycled ~500 times per day. The body must continuously regenerate ATP from ADP.

3.2 Photosynthesis

Photosynthesis is the process by which autotrophs (plants, algae, cyanobacteria) capture light energy and convert it into chemical energy stored in glucose. It occurs in the chloroplasts.

Overall Equation of Photosynthesis
6CO₂ + 6H₂O + Light Energy → C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂
(Carbon dioxide + Water + Light → Glucose + Oxygen)

Stage 1 — Light-Dependent Reactions (Thylakoid Membrane)

Occur in the thylakoid membranes. Capture light energy and convert it to chemical energy (ATP and NADPH). Also split water to produce oxygen as a byproduct.

  1. Chlorophyll and other pigments in Photosystem II (PSII) absorb light
  2. Light energy excites electrons to a higher energy level
  3. Water molecules are split (photolysis): 2H₂O → 4H⁺ + 4e⁻ + O₂ (oxygen released as byproduct)
  4. Excited electrons travel through the Electron Transport Chain (ETC), releasing energy used to pump H⁺ ions across the thylakoid membrane
  5. ATP synthase uses the H⁺ gradient to produce ATP (photophosphorylation)
  6. Electrons reach Photosystem I (PSI), are re-excited by light, and reduce NADP⁺ to NADPH
  7. Products of light reactions: ATP, NADPH, O₂

Stage 2 — Calvin Cycle / Dark Reactions (Stroma)

Occur in the stroma. Use ATP and NADPH from light reactions to build glucose from CO₂. Do NOT require light directly (though they are linked to light reactions for their inputs).

  1. Carbon fixation: CO₂ is attached to a 5-carbon molecule (RuBP) by the enzyme RuBisCO
  2. Reduction: ATP and NADPH are used to convert the 3-carbon intermediate (3-PGA) to G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate)
  3. Regeneration of RuBP: Some G3P is used to regenerate RuBP using ATP; cycle continues
  4. Some G3P exits the cycle and is used to build glucose and other organic molecules
  5. For every 3 CO₂ fixed: 3 ATP (per CO₂) and 2 NADPH needed; 1 G3P molecule produced net

Pigments & Light Absorption

  • Chlorophyll a: Primary pigment; absorbs blue-violet and red light; reflects green → plants appear green
  • Chlorophyll b: Accessory pigment; broadens range of light absorbed; passes energy to chlorophyll a
  • Carotenoids (β-carotene, xanthophylls): Yellow/orange pigments; absorb blue-green light; visible in autumn when chlorophyll breaks down

3.3 Cellular Respiration

Cellular respiration is the process by which cells break down organic molecules (primarily glucose) to release energy stored as ATP. All living organisms carry out cellular respiration.

Overall Equation of Aerobic Respiration
C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + ~36-38 ATP
(Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Energy)

Aerobic respiration occurs in THREE stages:

Stage 1 — Glycolysis (Cytoplasm)

  • Occurs in the cytoplasm — does NOT require oxygen (can occur in aerobic or anaerobic conditions)
  • One glucose (6C) is split into 2 pyruvate (3C each)
  • NET YIELD: 2 ATP, 2 NADH
  • 10 enzyme-catalyzed steps; glucose is first phosphorylated (uses 2 ATP) then broken down (produces 4 ATP = net 2)

Stage 2 — Krebs Cycle / Citric Acid Cycle (Mitochondrial Matrix)

  • Occurs in the mitochondrial matrix; requires oxygen (aerobic)
  • Pyruvate (2) → Acetyl-CoA (2) entering the cycle (releasing CO₂)
  • Acetyl-CoA + oxaloacetate → citric acid (6C) → series of reactions that release CO₂ and high-energy electrons (NADH, FADH₂)
  • YIELD (per glucose): 2 ATP, 6 NADH, 2 FADH₂, 4 CO₂

Stage 3 — Electron Transport Chain (ETC) & Oxidative Phosphorylation (Inner Mitochondrial Membrane)

  • Occurs on the inner mitochondrial membrane (cristae)
  • NADH and FADH₂ donate electrons to protein complexes in the ETC
  • Electrons pass through complexes I, II, III, IV, releasing energy that pumps H⁺ across the inner membrane (into intermembrane space)
  • H⁺ flows back through ATP synthase (chemiosmosis) → produces ~32-34 ATP
  • Final electron acceptor = O₂ → combines with H⁺ to form water (H₂O)
  • YIELD: ~32-34 ATP

Chlorophyll a, the primary photosynthetic pigment, absorbs light most strongly in the blue-violet (~430 nm) and red (~680 nm) wavelengths of the visible spectrum. In autumn, chlorophyll breaks down, revealing the yellow/orange carotenoid pigments that were masked. Some trees also produce red/purple anthocyanins as chlorophyll degrades.
Short Answer

10. Why is the inner mitochondrial membrane folded into cristae? What would happen to ATP production if the cristae were eliminated?

Model Answer
The inner mitochondrial membrane is folded into extensive finger-like projections called cristae to dramatically increase surface area. The electron transport chain (ETC) protein complexes and ATP synthase molecules are embedded in this membrane. A greater surface area means more ETC complexes and ATP synthase units can fit, allowing greater ATP production per mitochondrion.

If cristae were eliminated (replaced by a smooth inner membrane), the surface area would decrease by roughly 5-fold. This would dramatically reduce the number of ETC complexes and ATP synthase molecules, drastically cutting ATP production — the cell would lack sufficient energy for active processes and would likely die in energy-demanding tissues like muscle and brain.
Short Answer

11. Describe what happens to a plant kept in complete darkness for several days. Explain using your knowledge of photosynthesis and respiration.

Model Answer
In complete darkness, the plant cannot perform photosynthesis (no light energy to drive light reactions). However, the plant continues to carry out cellular respiration 24/7, consuming stored glucose and other organic molecules to produce ATP.

Initially: the plant draws on glucose stored in cells and starch reserves. O₂ is consumed and CO₂ is released. Chlorophyll begins to degrade (no light to maintain it), and leaves begin to yellow (etiolation). The plant cannot replace the glucose it is consuming because photosynthesis is absent. Leaves may begin to wilt as turgor pressure drops, and the plant eventually starves if kept in darkness long enough. If returned to light early, recovery is possible. Long-term darkness leads to death from starvation.
Essay

12. Trace a carbon atom from atmospheric CO₂ through photosynthesis and then through cellular respiration back to CO₂. At each step, describe what molecule the carbon atom is part of and what process it undergoes.

Model Answer
Step 1 — CO₂ in the atmosphere: The carbon atom exists as part of CO₂ (one carbon bonded to two oxygens).

Step 2 — Carbon fixation (Calvin Cycle, Stroma): CO₂ is captured by the enzyme RuBisCO and attached to a 5-carbon molecule, RuBP (ribulose bisphosphate), producing an unstable 6-carbon intermediate that immediately splits into two 3-carbon molecules of 3-PGA (3-phosphoglycerate). Our carbon atom is now in 3-PGA.

Step 3 — Reduction (Calvin Cycle): Using ATP and NADPH from the light reactions, 3-PGA is reduced to G3P (glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate). Our carbon is now in G3P.

Step 4 — Glucose synthesis: G3P molecules are combined to form glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆). Our carbon atom is now part of a glucose molecule — chemical energy stored in C-H bonds.

Step 5 — Glycolysis (Cytoplasm): Glucose is split into two pyruvate molecules (3C each). Our carbon is now in pyruvate. 2 ATP and 2 NADH are produced.

Step 6 — Pyruvate → Acetyl-CoA (Mitochondrial matrix): Pyruvate is decarboxylated (loses a CO₂!) and converted to acetyl-CoA (2C). Some carbons leave here as CO₂ — but let's say our carbon continues into the cycle.

Step 7 — Krebs Cycle (Matrix): Acetyl-CoA enters the cycle, carbons are oxidized, releasing CO₂ at two steps. After one full cycle, the original carbon atoms from glucose have all been released as CO₂.

Step 8 — CO₂ released: Our carbon atom is once again CO₂, exhaled and returned to the atmosphere to be captured again by photosynthesis.

Summary: CO₂ (atmosphere) → glucose (via Calvin cycle in chloroplast) → pyruvate (via glycolysis in cytoplasm) → acetyl-CoA → CO₂ (via Krebs cycle in mitochondria) → returned to atmosphere.

4.1 DNA — Structure & Replication

DNA (Deoxyribonucleic acid) is the molecule that carries genetic information in all living organisms. Its structure, discovered by Watson & Crick in 1953 (using X-ray crystallography data from Rosalind Franklin), is a double helix.

Components of DNA

  • Nucleotide: The monomer (building block) of DNA. Each nucleotide consists of:
    • A 5-carbon deoxyribose sugar
    • A phosphate group
    • A nitrogenous base (one of four: A, T, G, C)
  • Purine bases (double ring): Adenine (A), Guanine (G)
  • Pyrimidine bases (single ring): Thymine (T), Cytosine (C)
  • Base pairing rules (Chargaff's Rules): A pairs with T (2 hydrogen bonds); G pairs with C (3 hydrogen bonds)
  • Sugar-phosphate backbone: Covalent bonds between the phosphate of one nucleotide and the sugar of the next form the "backbone" of each strand
  • Antiparallel orientation: The two strands run in opposite directions (one 5'→3', the other 3'→5')

DNA Replication

DNA must be copied (replicated) before every cell division. Replication is semiconservative — each new double helix contains one original (template) strand and one new strand.

2. StabilizingSingle-strand binding proteins (SSBPs)Keep the two strands separated and stable

4.2 Gene Expression — Transcription & Translation

Genes are segments of DNA that encode proteins. The process of converting genetic information into a functional protein has two major steps: Transcription (DNA → RNA) and Translation (RNA → Protein). This is summarized by the Central Dogma of Biology: DNA → RNA → Protein.

Transcription (Nucleus → mRNA)

  1. RNA polymerase binds to the promoter region of a gene on the DNA
  2. The DNA double helix unwinds; RNA polymerase reads the template strand (3'→5') and synthesizes a complementary mRNA strand (5'→3')
  3. RNA uses U (uracil) instead of T (thymine); A pairs with U; G pairs with C
  4. The mRNA is processed (5' cap, poly-A tail added; introns removed by spliceosomes; exons joined)
  5. Mature mRNA exits the nucleus through nuclear pores

Translation (Ribosome → Protein)

  1. mRNA binds to a ribosome (at the start codon AUG — methionine)
  2. tRNA molecules carry specific amino acids; each tRNA has an anticodon that base-pairs with the mRNA codon
  3. As the ribosome moves along the mRNA (5'→3'), tRNAs bring amino acids; peptide bonds form between amino acids, building a polypeptide chain
  4. When a stop codon (UAA, UAG, UGA) is reached, translation ends and the polypeptide is released
  5. The polypeptide folds into a functional protein
The Genetic Code
DNA uses a triplet code — every 3 nucleotides (called a codon in mRNA) codes for one amino acid.
There are 64 possible codons (4³ = 64) but only 20 amino acids → the code is degenerate (multiple codons can code for the same amino acid).
Start codon: AUG (methionine)
Stop codons: UAA, UAG, UGA (do not code for amino acids)

Sources of Genetic Variation in Meiosis

  • Crossing over (Prophase I): Homologous chromosomes physically exchange segments at points called chiasmata, creating new combinations of alleles (recombinant chromosomes)
  • Independent assortment (Metaphase I): Homologous chromosome pairs align randomly at the metaphase plate; each daughter cell gets a random mix of maternal and paternal chromosomes. With 23 pairs in humans: 2²³ ≈ 8 million possible combinations.
  • Random fertilization: Any sperm (~8 million combinations) can fertilize any egg (~8 million combinations) → >64 trillion possible offspring combinations

4.4 Mendelian Genetics

Gregor Mendel (1822–1884), an Austrian monk, discovered the basic principles of heredity through careful experiments with pea plants. He is considered the "Father of Genetics."

Key Vocabulary

Gene
A segment of DNA that codes for a protein (and ultimately a trait). Genes occupy specific loci (positions) on chromosomes.
Allele
Different versions of the same gene (e.g., "B" for brown eyes, "b" for blue eyes).
Homozygous
Having two identical alleles for a gene (BB or bb); also called "true breeding" or "pure breeding."
Heterozygous
Having two different alleles for a gene (Bb); also called a "hybrid."
Genotype
The genetic makeup of an organism (the actual alleles it has, e.g., BB, Bb, or bb).
Phenotype
The observable physical expression of the genotype (e.g., brown hair, blood type O, tall plant).
Dominant
An allele that is expressed in the phenotype when even one copy is present; represented by a capital letter (B).
Recessive
An allele whose phenotype is masked by the dominant allele; only expressed when homozygous (bb).

Mendel's Laws

  • Law of Segregation: Each organism has two alleles for each trait; alleles separate during gamete formation so each gamete carries only one allele. During fertilization, two gametes combine to restore the pair.
  • Law of Independent Assortment: Genes for different traits are distributed to gametes independently of each other (applies to genes on different chromosomes or far apart on the same chromosome).

Punnett Squares — Monohybrid Cross

Example: Tall (T) is dominant over short (t). Cross: Tt × Tt

4.5 Mutations

A mutation is a change in the DNA sequence. Mutations can be spontaneous (random errors during replication) or induced (caused by mutagens — UV light, chemicals, radiation).

Types of Gene Mutations

Substitution (point mutation)One base replaces anotherMay change one amino acid (missense), create a stop codon (nonsense), or have no effect (silent)Sickle cell anemia (GAG → GTG changes glutamate → valine in hemoglobin)

DeletionBase(s) removed from the sequenceFrameshift (if not in multiples of 3); all downstream codons alteredCystic fibrosis (deletion of 3 bases in CFTR gene)

Chromosomal Mutations

  • Deletion: A segment of a chromosome is lost
  • Duplication: A segment is repeated
  • Inversion: A segment is reversed within the chromosome
  • Translocation: A segment moves to a non-homologous chromosome
  • Nondisjunction: Failure of chromosomes to separate during meiosis → aneuploidy (wrong number of chromosomes). Example: Trisomy 21 (Down syndrome) — 3 copies of chromosome 21
Are All Mutations Bad?
No. Most mutations are neutral (no effect on fitness). Some are harmful (cause genetic diseases). A small fraction are beneficial (increase fitness in the current environment — the raw material for evolution). Mutations in somatic cells affect only that individual; mutations in germ cells (eggs/sperm) can be passed to offspring.

Unit 4 Key Takeaways

  • DNA is a double helix; base pairing: A-T (2 bonds), G-C (3 bonds); antiparallel strands
  • Central Dogma: DNA → (transcription) → mRNA → (translation) → Protein
  • Meiosis produces 4 haploid cells with genetic variation (crossing over + independent assortment)
  • Mendel's Laws: Segregation (alleles separate) and Independent Assortment (genes on different chromosomes sort independently)
  • Non-Mendelian patterns: incomplete dominance (blend), codominance (both expressed), multiple alleles, polygenic, sex-linked
  • Mutations: substitution (may be silent, missense, or nonsense); insertion/deletion = frameshifts

9. Explain the difference between genotype and phenotype. Give an example where two individuals have the same phenotype but different genotypes.

11. Trace the journey of a protein from the DNA that encodes it to its final destination outside the cell. Include all steps, molecules, and organelles involved.

Model Answer
Step 1 — DNA (Nucleus): The gene for the protein exists as a sequence of nucleotides in the DNA double helix within the nucleus. Regulatory proteins (transcription factors) bind to the promoter region and recruit RNA polymerase.

Step 2 — Transcription (Nucleus): RNA polymerase unwinds the DNA and reads the template strand (3'→5'), synthesizing a complementary pre-mRNA strand (5'→3'). RNA uses U instead of T. The pre-mRNA is then processed: a 5' cap and poly-A tail are added (for stability and ribosome recognition), and introns (non-coding sequences) are removed by spliceosomes, leaving only exons (coding sequences) in the mature mRNA.

Step 3 — mRNA export: The mature mRNA exits the nucleus through nuclear pores into the cytoplasm.

Step 4 — Translation (Rough ER / Ribosome): The mRNA is recognized by a ribosome. For secreted proteins, the ribosome docks on the rough ER. The ribosome reads the mRNA codons (triplets) in the 5'→3' direction. tRNA molecules with complementary anticodons bring specific amino acids. Peptide bonds form between amino acids as the ribosome moves along the mRNA. The growing polypeptide is threaded into the ER lumen. Translation stops at a stop codon (UAA, UAG, or UGA).

Step 5 — ER processing: Inside the ER lumen, the polypeptide folds into its 3D shape with the help of chaperone proteins. Initial modifications are made (e.g., glycosylation — addition of carbohydrate chains). The protein is packaged into a transport vesicle.

Step 6 — Golgi Apparatus: The transport vesicle fuses with the cis face of the Golgi. The protein moves through Golgi cisternae, receiving further modifications (additional glycosylation, phosphorylation). It is sorted and tagged for its final destination and exits via a vesicle from the trans face.

Step 7 — Secretion (Exocytosis): The secretory vesicle moves to the plasma membrane and fuses with it (exocytosis), releasing the protein outside the cell into the extracellular space.

5.3 Respiratory System

The respiratory system brings O₂ into the body and removes CO₂. Gas exchange occurs in the lungs.

Pathway of Air

Nose/Mouth → Pharynx → Larynx → Trachea → Bronchi → Bronchioles → Alveoli (site of gas exchange)

Gas Exchange at Alveoli

  • Alveoli are tiny air sacs with extremely thin walls (1 cell thick) and surrounded by capillaries
  • Large surface area (~75 m² in adult human) and short diffusion distance maximize exchange efficiency
  • O₂ diffuses from alveoli (high [O₂]) → capillary blood (low [O₂]); binds to hemoglobin in RBCs
  • CO₂ diffuses from blood (high [CO₂]) → alveoli (low [CO₂]); exhaled

Breathing Mechanics

  • Inhalation: Diaphragm contracts + external intercostals contract → thoracic volume increases → pressure decreases → air flows IN
  • Exhalation: Diaphragm relaxes + internal intercostals contract → thoracic volume decreases → pressure increases → air flows OUT
  • Breathing is controlled by the medulla oblongata, which monitors blood CO₂ levels

5.5 Nervous System

The nervous system provides rapid communication throughout the body using electrical and chemical signals.

Organization

  • CNS (Central Nervous System): Brain + Spinal cord — integration and processing center
  • PNS (Peripheral Nervous System): All nerves outside CNS
    • Somatic NS: Voluntary control of skeletal muscles
    • Autonomic NS: Involuntary control (sympathetic = "fight or flight"; parasympathetic = "rest and digest")

The Neuron — Structure & Function

  • Dendrites: Receive signals from other neurons or sensory receptors
  • Cell body (soma): Contains nucleus; integrates incoming signals
  • Axon: Carries signals away from cell body; long process that can extend over a meter
  • Myelin sheath: Fatty insulation (from Schwann cells in PNS, oligodendrocytes in CNS) that speeds up signal conduction (saltatory conduction)
  • Axon terminals / synaptic knobs: Release neurotransmitters into the synapse

The Action Potential

  1. Resting state: Inside negative (~-70 mV) due to Na⁺/K⁺ pump
  2. Depolarization: Stimulus causes Na⁺ channels to open → Na⁺ rushes in → inside becomes positive
  3. Repolarization: K⁺ channels open → K⁺ rushes out → inside returns to negative
  4. Hyperpolarization: Briefly more negative than resting; refractory period
  5. Na⁺/K⁺ pump restores original ion concentrations

The Synapse

  • Gap between two neurons (presynaptic → synaptic cleft → postsynaptic)
  • Action potential arrives at axon terminal → Ca²⁺ channels open → vesicles release neurotransmitters (e.g., acetylcholine, dopamine, serotonin) into cleft
  • Neurotransmitters bind to receptors on postsynaptic membrane → new signal generated (or inhibited)
  • Neurotransmitters are then broken down or reabsorbed

5.7 Immune System

The immune system defends the body against pathogens (bacteria, viruses, parasites, fungi) and other threats.

Lines of Defense

  • 1st Line (Innate, Non-specific — physical/chemical barriers): Skin; mucous membranes; stomach acid; tears; cilia; normal microbiota
  • 2nd Line (Innate, Non-specific — internal defenses): Inflammation (histamine, vasodilation); fever; natural killer cells; phagocytosis (macrophages, neutrophils)
  • 3rd Line (Adaptive, Specific — acquired immunity):
    • Humoral immunity: B lymphocytes → plasma cells → produce antibodies specific to antigens
    • Cell-mediated immunity: T lymphocytes — Helper T cells (activate B cells and cytotoxic T cells); Cytotoxic T cells (kill infected or cancerous cells)
    • Memory cells: Long-lived B and T memory cells remain after infection for rapid response upon re-exposure (basis of vaccination)

Vaccines — How They Work

Vaccines introduce weakened, killed, or component antigens of a pathogen. The immune system mounts a response and produces memory B and T cells. If the real pathogen is encountered later, the immune system can respond rapidly and powerfully — preventing or reducing disease severity.

Unit 5 Key Takeaways

  • The circulatory system is a closed double circuit: pulmonary (heart ↔ lungs) + systemic (heart ↔ body)
  • Gas exchange occurs at alveoli (respiratory) and capillaries (systemic); driven by diffusion
  • Digestion breaks macromolecules into monomers for absorption; small intestine is the main absorption site
  • Neurons transmit electrical signals (action potentials); synapses use chemical neurotransmitters
  • Endocrine system uses hormones for slow, sustained regulation; pancreas regulates blood glucose via insulin/glucagon
  • Immune system: 3 lines of defense; adaptive immunity (B cells → antibodies; T cells → cell killing); memory cells = basis of vaccination

1. Which chamber of the heart pumps oxygenated blood to the body?

  • Right atrium
  • Right ventricle
  • Left atrium
  • Left ventricle
Answer
D — Left ventricle. The left ventricle pumps oxygenated blood (received from the left atrium, which received it from the pulmonary veins coming from the lungs) through the aorta to the systemic circuit (body). It has the thickest, most muscular walls of any chamber because it must generate the highest pressure. The right ventricle pumps deoxygenated blood to the lungs (pulmonary circuit) via the pulmonary artery.
Multiple Choice

2. Where does gas exchange (O₂/CO₂) occur in the respiratory system?

  • Trachea
  • Bronchi
  • Alveoli
  • Larynx
Answer
C — Alveoli. Alveoli are tiny, thin-walled air sacs at the ends of the bronchiole tree. They are surrounded by a dense network of capillaries. Their features — single-cell-thick walls, moist surfaces (for gas dissolution), and enormous total surface area (~75 m²) — make them the ideal site for O₂ and CO₂ exchange by simple diffusion. The trachea, bronchi, and larynx are conducting airways (transport only, no gas exchange).
Multiple Choice

3. Which organ produces bile to emulsify fats?

  • Stomach
  • Pancreas
  • Small intestine
  • Liver
Answer
D — Liver. The liver produces bile, which is stored in the gallbladder and released into the small intestine (duodenum) via the bile duct. Bile does not chemically digest fats — it physically emulsifies them, breaking large fat droplets into smaller ones (like soap on grease), increasing the surface area for lipase (from the pancreas) to chemically digest. The pancreas produces digestive enzymes (amylase, lipase, proteases); the stomach produces pepsin and HCl.
Multiple Choice

4. Which part of the neuron carries signals AWAY from the cell body?

  • Dendrites
  • Nucleus
  • Axon
  • Myelin sheath
Answer
C — Axon. The axon is the long process that conducts action potentials away from the cell body toward the axon terminals, where neurotransmitters are released. Dendrites bring signals TO the cell body. The myelin sheath insulates the axon and speeds up signal transmission (saltatory conduction). Remember the direction: Dendrite → Cell body → Axon (like traffic flowing to and from a central hub).
Multiple Choice

5. Insulin is produced by the pancreas and lowers blood glucose. Glucagon raises blood glucose. This is an example of:

  • Positive feedback
  • Negative feedback
  • Active transport
  • Immune response
Answer
B — Negative feedback. Negative feedback loops oppose or reverse a change from the set point, bringing conditions back to normal. When blood glucose rises above normal → insulin released → glucose taken up → blood glucose falls back to normal. When blood glucose falls too low → glucagon released → liver releases glucose → blood glucose rises. Each response counteracts (reverses) the original change — this is the hallmark of negative feedback, the primary mechanism of homeostasis.
Multiple Choice

6. Vaccines protect against future infections primarily by:

  • Destroying all pathogens in the bloodstream immediately
  • Creating memory B and T cells that allow rapid immune response upon re-exposure
  • Increasing the number of red blood cells
  • Strengthening the skin barrier
Answer
B. Vaccines stimulate the adaptive immune system to produce a primary immune response against a harmless version of an antigen, generating antigen-specific memory B cells (that can rapidly produce antibodies) and memory T cells. If the real pathogen is encountered later, the secondary immune response is much faster and stronger than the primary — often clearing the infection before symptoms develop. This principle is called immunological memory.
Short Answer

7. Explain how the structure of capillaries makes them ideally suited for their function of material exchange.

Model Answer
Capillaries are the site of exchange between blood and tissues, and their structure is perfectly matched to this function:

1. Single cell thick walls (endothelium only): The short diffusion distance allows rapid exchange of O₂, CO₂, glucose, amino acids, and waste products.
2. Enormous number: Capillaries are so abundant that no body cell is more than ~0.1 mm from a capillary, ensuring efficient delivery and collection.
3. Total surface area: The combined surface area of all capillaries (~6,000 m²) maximizes exchange area.
4. Slow blood flow: Blood moves more slowly through capillaries (due to their large total cross-sectional area) than in arteries, allowing more time for exchange.
5. Fenestrations (pores): Some capillaries (in kidney glomerulus, small intestine) have pores that allow larger molecules to pass.
Short Answer

8. Compare and contrast the nervous system and the endocrine system in terms of speed, duration, and type of signal. Give a specific example of each.

Model Answer
Nervous System: — Signal type: Electrical (action potentials) transmitted along neurons; chemical only at synapses (neurotransmitters) — Speed: Extremely fast (milliseconds to seconds) — Duration: Short-lived (effect lasts only while neurotransmitters are bound) — Target: Specific cells/tissues via neural connections — Example: When you touch a hot stove, sensory neurons carry a signal to the spinal cord in milliseconds → reflex arc → motor neurons signal muscles to withdraw your hand immediately.

Endocrine System: — Signal type: Chemical (hormones) released into bloodstream — Speed: Slow (seconds to hours; must travel through blood) — Duration: Long-lasting (effect continues while hormone is in blood — minutes to days) — Target: Broad — any cells with the appropriate receptor (often widespread effects) — Example: During puberty, the pituitary gland releases GnRH/FSH/LH, stimulating the gonads to produce testosterone or estrogen over years, causing gradual development of secondary sex characteristics.
Essay

9. Trace the journey of a meal from your mouth to your bloodstream. Include all organs and enzymes involved, and explain how the structure of the small intestine maximizes absorption.

Model Answer
Mouth: Chewing (mechanical digestion) breaks food into smaller pieces, increasing surface area. Salivary glands secrete saliva containing salivary amylase, which begins hydrolysis of starch into maltose. Food is formed into a bolus.

Esophagus: The bolus is swallowed and moved to the stomach by peristalsis (rhythmic muscular contractions of smooth muscle). No digestion occurs here.

Stomach: The stomach churns food (mechanical digestion) and secretes gastric juice (pH ~2): hydrochloric acid (HCl) denatures proteins and kills microbes; pepsinogen is activated to pepsin by HCl, beginning protein digestion. After 2–4 hours, the partially digested food (chyme) is released into the small intestine through the pyloric sphincter.

Small Intestine (Duodenum): Chyme is neutralized by bicarbonate from the pancreas. The pancreas releases a suite of enzymes: pancreatic amylase (starch → maltose), pancreatic lipase (fats → fatty acids + glycerol), and proteases (trypsin, chymotrypsin — proteins → peptides; peptidases → amino acids). The liver provides bile (via bile duct) to emulsify fat droplets, increasing their surface area for lipase action. Intestinal enzymes on the brush border complete digestion (e.g., maltase breaks maltose → 2 glucose; peptidases → amino acids).

Absorption (Jejunum and Ileum of small intestine): The small intestine (~6 m long) is specialized for maximum absorption: — Villi: Finger-like projections of the mucosa that increase surface area — Microvilli (brush border): Microscopic extensions on epithelial cells of villi, further multiplying surface area (~200 m² total) — Rich blood and lymph supply: Each villus contains a capillary network and a lacteal (lymph vessel) Monosaccharides (glucose, fructose) and amino acids are absorbed by transport proteins into capillaries → portal vein → liver (for processing). Fatty acids and glycerol enter epithelial cells, are reassembled into triglycerides, packaged into chylomicrons, and absorbed into lacteals → lymphatic system → eventually drain into blood via the thoracic duct.

Large intestine: Remaining water and electrolytes are absorbed; feces are compacted and ultimately eliminated.
